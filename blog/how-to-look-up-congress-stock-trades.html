How to Look Up Congress Stock Trades in the Official Disclosures

September 2026 · 9 min read · Finance

Few corners of investing attract as much curiosity as the stock trades of members of Congress. Lawmakers sit on committees that shape regulation, hand out contracts and receive briefings the public never sees, so it is natural to wonder what they buy and sell. The good news is that their trades are public record. You do not need a paid tracker to see them; you need to know where the official filings live and how to read them.

This guide covers the rules that require disclosure, the two official databases, how to read a transaction report, what third-party trackers add, what the research says about performance and where reform stands in September 2026.

What the STOCK Act Requires

The Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act of 2012, known as the STOCK Act, made it explicit that members of Congress are not exempt from insider trading laws and set out how quickly they must report their trades. The key rules:

  • Who: members of the House and Senate and their senior staff, for transactions made by themselves, their spouses and their dependent children.
  • What: purchases, sales and exchanges of stocks, bonds, commodity futures and other securities worth more than $1,000. Widely held, diversified investment funds are generally exempt.
  • When: in a periodic transaction report, filed within 30 days of learning about the transaction and no later than 45 days after it took place.
  • Penalty: a late filing usually costs $200. Critics point out that for a large trade this is trivial, and news investigations have found many members filing late.

Separately, members file an annual financial disclosure report, due each May, that lists their holdings, income and liabilities. Transaction reports tell you what changed; annual reports tell you what they own.

Where to Find the Filings

House of Representatives

The Clerk of the House publishes financial disclosure reports on its disclosures website (disclosures-clerk.house.gov). You can search by name, state, district and filing year. Transaction reports are labelled PTR in the results. Recent filings are usually searchable PDFs; some older or paper-filed reports are scanned images, which is why they can be slow to read.

Senate

The Senate's electronic financial disclosure system, known as eFD (efdsearch.senate.gov), asks you to confirm that you understand the legal restrictions on using the reports before you search. Federal law prohibits using them for any unlawful purpose, for commercial purposes other than news reporting, for credit ratings or for soliciting money. You can then filter by senator, date range and report type, choosing "Periodic Transactions" to see trades. Electronically filed reports display as web pages; paper filings appear as images.

How to Read a Periodic Transaction Report

A transaction report is short, but each field matters:

  • Owner: whether the asset belongs to the member, a spouse (often SP), a joint account (JT) or a dependent child (DC). A large share of the most discussed trades are made by spouses.
  • Asset: the security's name and usually its ticker. Watch for options, which are reported with their own details and carry very different risk from the shares themselves.
  • Transaction type: purchase, full sale, partial sale or exchange.
  • Dates: the transaction date (and, on House reports, the date the member was notified). The gap between these and the filing date tells you how stale the information is by the time you see it.
  • Amount: a range, not an exact figure. The bands run from $1,001–$15,000 and $15,001–$50,000 up to $1,000,001–$5,000,000 and beyond, so a single entry can mean a trade of $2,000 or $14,000.

Some members use advisers who trade on their behalf, and filings sometimes say so. That is worth noting before reading intent into any single transaction.

Common Misreadings

Most viral claims about congressional trades fall apart on one of a few points:

  • Options are not shares. A call option with a distant expiry date and a high strike price is a very different bet from buying the stock, and a small premium can control a large notional position.
  • Ranges are not amounts. A trade in the $1,000,001–$5,000,000 band could be one million or nearly five.
  • Sales are not always signals. Members sell for the same reasons as everyone else: diversification, tax, a house purchase. A single sale says little.
  • A late filing is not proof of wrongdoing. It is a breach of the reporting rules, which matters, but it does not by itself show that the trade used non-public information.

Connecting Trades to Committees

The question most people really want answered is whether a trade relates to a member's official work. Committee and subcommittee assignments are listed on each member's page on congress.gov and on the committees' own websites, and hearing schedules show what a committee was working on at the time. A trade in a defence contractor by a member of an armed services committee, a week before a relevant hearing, is worth a closer look; the same trade by a member with no related role is far less interesting.

Trackers are convenient, but they are only as good as their parsing. Ranges get misread, spouses become members, and options become shares. Before you draw a conclusion from a tracker, open the original report it came from.

What the Research Says

The academic evidence is more mixed than the headlines suggest. A widely cited 2004 study by Alan Ziobrowski and colleagues found that senators' stock picks from 1993 to 1998 beat the market by roughly 12% a year. After the STOCK Act, the picture changed: a 2022 study in the Journal of Public Economics by Belmont, Sacerdote, Sehgal and Van Hoek examined trades from 2012 to 2020 and found no evidence of superior performance, with returns consistent with random stock picking. More recently, a 2025 working paper by Shang-Jin Wei and Yifan Zhou found that the small group of members who rose to leadership positions went on to outperform comparable colleagues by a wide margin after taking those posts.

The practical lesson is that congressional trades as a whole are not a reliable signal, and even where outperformance exists, the disclosure lag of up to 45 days means you see the trade long after it happened. Copying individual trades is not a strategy.

Where Reform Stands

Proposals to ban individual stock trading by members of Congress have circulated for years. In July 2026 the House passed H.R. 7008, the Stop Insider Trading Act, by 232 votes to 198. It would bar members, their spouses and their dependent children from buying individual stocks and require public notice before selling existing holdings. Critics said it did not go far enough, and an unrelated voter identification provision attached to it cost it Democratic support. At the end of September 2026 the bill was awaiting action in the Senate, where its prospects were uncertain. Congress.gov has the current status of every bill.

Corporate Insiders Are Different

Congressional trades are often confused with insider trades by company executives, which follow very different rules. Officers, directors and large shareholders report their transactions on Form 4 with the SEC, usually within two business days, and the filings appear on EDGAR. The transaction codes tell you a lot: P is an open-market purchase, S an open-market sale, A a grant or award, M an option exercise and F shares withheld to pay tax. Open-market purchases are the ones investors watch most closely, because executives sell for many reasons but usually buy for one.

A Practical Checklist

  1. Start from the official report, even if you found the trade through a tracker or a social media post.
  2. Check the owner, the dates and the range before assuming anything about size or timing.
  3. Look for patterns rather than single trades: several members buying the same company, or a member trading in an industry their committee oversees.
  4. Read the company's own filings before forming a view on the business itself.
  5. Remember the lag. By the time a trade is public, the price has had weeks to move.

The Bottom Line

Congressional trading data is public, and it is worth knowing how to read it, both as a citizen and as an investor. Use the official House and Senate databases, treat trackers as a convenient index rather than the source, and resist the temptation to copy trades that are weeks old. The disclosures are most useful as a prompt to research a company properly, not as a shortcut around doing so.
