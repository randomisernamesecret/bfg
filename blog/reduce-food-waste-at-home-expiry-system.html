How to Stop Wasting Food at Home: A Simple Expiry-Date System That Works

September 2026 · 7 min read · Home

Most food waste doesn't feel like waste while it's happening. It feels like a yoghurt pushed to the back of the fridge, a bag of spinach you were definitely going to use, a second jar of passata bought because you couldn't remember whether you had one. Nobody decides to throw food away. It just quietly expires while you're busy.

That's why "try harder" rarely works. What works is a small system that does the remembering for you. This guide walks through one that takes a few minutes a week, costs nothing to start, and fits a real household rather than an idealised one.

How much food actually gets wasted at home

The numbers are larger than most people expect. The UN Environment Programme's Food Waste Index Report 2024 estimates that around 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted worldwide in 2022, and that households were responsible for roughly 60% of it, about 79 kg per person per year. In the UK, the charity WRAP estimates that households throw away around 6.4 million tonnes of food a year, most of which could have been eaten.

The money follows the food. WRAP puts the cost for an average family of four at around £1,000 a year. You don't need to believe any single figure exactly to see the point: the fridge is one of the most expensive bins in the house.

Why food gets wasted: the four usual culprits

  • Out of sight, out of mind. Food at the back of the fridge or the top of the cupboard gets forgotten. Most waste is caused by visibility, not carelessness.
  • Date confusion. People throw away food that is still perfectly good because they misread a "best before" date as a safety deadline.
  • Buying what you already have. Without a clear picture of what's at home, the shop becomes guesswork, and duplicates pile up.
  • No plan for what's about to expire. You know the chicken needs using, but dinner gets decided at 7pm by whoever is hungriest.

Every step of the system below targets one of these four.

Step 1: Learn the two dates that matter

Before any system, get the labels right. In the UK and EU, food carries one of two dates, and they mean very different things:

LabelWhat it meansWhat to do
Use byA safety date. Found on foods that go off quickly, such as fresh meat, fish and some dairy.Eat, cook or freeze by the end of this date. Don't eat it after, even if it looks and smells fine.
Best beforeA quality date. Found on longer-life foods such as tins, pasta, crisps and many frozen foods.Food is usually safe after this date but may be past its best. Check how it looks, smells and has been stored.

The European Commission has estimated that up to 10% of the food wasted in the EU each year is linked to confusion about date marking. In the US, dates are less standardised: outside infant formula, "best if used by", "sell by" and "use by" are mostly manufacturer guidance about quality rather than federal safety rules. Wherever you live, the principle is the same: treat safety dates strictly and quality dates with common sense.

One exception worth remembering: storage instructions still apply. A "best before" date assumes the product was stored as the label says. Once opened, many foods have their own shorter window, such as "eat within three days of opening".

Step 2: Make an "eat first" zone

The single most effective change costs nothing: pick one shelf in the fridge and one spot in the cupboard, and put anything that needs using soon there. Leftovers, opened jars, the vegetables that are starting to soften. Everyone in the house learns one rule: if you're looking for a snack or planning a meal, start with the eat-first zone.

Pair it with first in, first out. When you unpack the shopping, move older items to the front and put new ones behind them. Supermarkets do this for a reason.

Step 3: Keep a simple inventory

A system only works if you can see what you have without opening every door. You don't need a spreadsheet. You need three facts per item: what it is, when it expires, and roughly how much is left.

There are a few ways to keep that list:

  • A whiteboard on the fridge. Cheap and visible. It works well for fresh food, less well for the cupboard and freezer, and it doesn't remind you.
  • A notes app or spreadsheet. Flexible, but typing every item is tedious, and most people give up within a fortnight.
  • An inventory app that scans. Scanning a barcode and tapping a date is fast enough to keep up with a real weekly shop, and the app can remind you before something goes off.

Whichever you choose, the test is simple: can you tell, in ten seconds, what needs using this week?

Step 4: A five-minute weekly check

Once a week, ideally the day before you shop, spend five minutes on this:

  1. Look at everything that expires in the next seven days.
  2. Decide what happens to each item: eat it, cook it, freeze it or share it.
  3. Plan two or three meals around those items first.
  4. Write the shopping list after the plan, not before.

Step four is where the savings are. A shopping list written after you've seen what's at home stops the duplicate passata and the third bag of carrots.

Step 5: Cook from the shelf, not from scratch

Most recipe searches start with "what do I want to eat?" Try starting with "what do I need to use?" Search by ingredient instead of by dish. A pot of soup, a frittata, a stir-fry or a tray bake will take almost any vegetable that's past its crispest.

A few reliable rescues:

  • Soft vegetables: soup, curry, pasta sauce or a tray bake.
  • Stale bread: breadcrumbs, croutons, bread pudding or French toast.
  • Overripe bananas: freeze them peeled for smoothies or banana bread.
  • Leftover rice: cool it quickly, refrigerate it and use it within a day, for example as fried rice.
  • Milk near its date: pancakes, white sauce, rice pudding, or freeze it.

Step 6: Use your freezer as a pause button

Freezing stops the clock. Most fresh meat, bread, cooked dishes, grated cheese and many fruits freeze well. For "use by" foods, freeze before the end of the date, then defrost in the fridge and use within 24 hours. Label what you freeze with the date. A freezer full of unidentified tubs is just a colder way to waste food.

Step 7: Get the household on the same page

Food waste is a team sport. One person can run a perfect system while someone else buys a second block of cheddar because they didn't know there was one. Three habits help:

  • One shared shopping list that anyone can add to when something runs out.
  • A visible "running low" signal, so nobody is surprised by the empty milk carton.
  • Decide dinner together, early. A quick vote in the afternoon beats a debate at 7pm that ends in a takeaway while the chicken expires.

Fridge basics that make everything last longer

  • Keep the fridge cold. The UK Food Standards Agency recommends 5°C or below. In the US, the FDA recommends 4°C (40°F) or below. A cheap fridge thermometer tells you whether yours is.
  • Don't overfill it. Cold air needs room to circulate.
  • Store raw meat on the bottom shelf, covered, so it can't drip onto other food.
  • Keep bread out of the fridge unless you want it stale faster. Freeze it instead.
  • Give herbs a glass of water, like cut flowers, and they'll last days longer.

The bottom line

Wasting less food isn't about willpower. It's about seeing what you have, knowing what the dates really mean, and deciding what to eat before the food decides for you. Start with an eat-first shelf and a five-minute weekly check. Add a simple inventory once the habit sticks. Most households notice the difference in the bin, and on the receipt, within a month.
