Flo vs Clue vs Ondi: Period Tracker Privacy Compared (2026)

Updated 11 November 2026 · 6 min read · Health

Period tracking apps hold some of the most personal information on your phone: cycle dates, symptoms, mood, sleep and, for some people, things they have never told anyone. Choosing where that record lives is as important as choosing which features you want.

This comparison looks at three apps that answer that question differently. Flo and Clue are two of the best-known period trackers. Ondi, which we make, is a personal cycle diary that keeps your logs on your iPhone or iPad. We describe Flo and Clue only from what they and the public record say, and we point you to their current policies for the rest, because policies and prices change.

Why the Storage Model Matters More Than the Privacy Policy

Cycle data can reveal very personal things. In places where reproductive healthcare is legally restricted, that information can have consequences far beyond embarrassment, and even where it is not, a leak of cycle data is a different kind of harm from a leak of your step count.

The public record shows why it is worth checking. In January 2021 the US Federal Trade Commission announced a complaint against Flo Health, alleging that the company shared users’ health information with outside data analytics providers after promising to keep it private; a final order followed in June 2021 (see the FTC case page). That is history, not a description of how Flo works today, and we make no claim about that: Flo’s current privacy policy does.

What it does show is that a privacy policy is a promise made by a company, and a promise can be changed or outlived by the business that made it. The more useful question is where your data physically sits, and who could be asked to hand it over.

Flo

Flo is one of the best-known period trackers. How it stores, uses and shares your data, which features are free and what the paid plan costs are all set out on Flo’s own pages and in its App Store listing, and they change. Read Flo’s current privacy policy and the app’s privacy label rather than relying on a comparison written by a competitor.

Clue

Clue is made by BioWink GmbH in Berlin. Its privacy policy (last updated 29 September 2026 when we read it) says all of its data is stored on servers in the European Union, with a few listed exceptions such as website analytics and integrations you choose; that your personal profile data is stored separately from your health data; and that the health data you track is never shared with or sold to advertisers. It also says that, if you allow it in your privacy settings, de-identified health data can be shared with research partners. For many people that research is a reason to choose Clue; if you want no copy of your data anywhere but your own device, it is a point to weigh. Check the current policy for the details and for your rights over your data.

Ondi: A Personal Cycle Diary on Your Device

Ondi is a personal cycle diary. It shows estimates worked out from what you log, so you can see your own patterns. There is no account to create, no Ondi server and no tracking analytics. Your logs are stored on your device; they are included in your device’s own backups, and anything you export goes only where you send it. Saving periods to Apple Health is off until you turn it on.

Free: period tracking with automatic cycle detection, daily logging of flow, mood, energy, sleep and symptoms, temperature, cervical mucus and ovulation tests as notes, a next-period estimate with a range and how sure it is, a calendar with your cycle history, and export of your own data. Everything is open for the first seven days. After that, logging, history and the calendar stay free, and a one-time purchase, with no subscription, unlocks insights and patterns in what you logged, Ask Ondi on supported devices, smart ring insights with a supported smart ring, and pregnancy, postpartum and post-menopause dashboards in plain language.

A few details follow the same thinking. Lock Screen widgets and notifications keep personal details out of sight by default, and on supported devices Ask Ondi answers a question about your own logs in plain language, on your device.

Ondi is a personal cycle diary. It is not a medical device and not contraception. It is not intended to help you become pregnant or to avoid pregnancy, or to diagnose, monitor or treat any condition. Estimates come from what you log and can be wrong. Talk to a doctor about your health.

What Each Model Means in Practice

An app that keeps your history on its company’s servers can usually bring your history to a new phone, and its features can draw on the data it holds. The trade is that a company holds records you cannot see, so where those servers are, what the policy allows and what the law requires of the company all matter. Read the policy for the app you are considering, and re-read it from time to time.

An on-device diary has no server of its own for your logs. Reading them takes your unlocked device, and your device backups follow the settings you choose. The trade is that you look after your own exports and backups.

Worth considering: These are different models, not a ranking. Whatever you choose, read the app’s current privacy policy and its App Store privacy label. For a broader look at how to judge any tracker’s data practices, see our guide to period tracker privacy.

Which One Should You Choose?

Choose an app that keeps your history on the company’s servers if:

You want your history on any phone you sign in to, and you are comfortable with the company’s current policy and the law that applies to it. Read Flo’s and Clue’s policies and compare them with what matters to you.

Choose Ondi if:

You want a personal cycle diary whose logs stay on your device, with no account and no Ondi server. Logging, history and the calendar are free; a one-time unlock adds insights, Ask Ondi, smart ring insights and the pregnancy, postpartum and post-menopause dashboards. If you are moving from another app, our guide to switching period trackers explains how to bring your history with you.

The Bottom Line

There is no single right answer. Choosing deliberately matters more than choosing a particular app. Read the privacy policy, check the App Store privacy label, and ask one simple question of any app you are considering: if the company were breached, sold or served with a court order tomorrow, what could it hand over? Your answer is the best guide to which tracker fits you.
