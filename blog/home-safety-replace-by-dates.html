Smoke Alarms, CO Detectors and First-Aid Kits: The Replace-By Dates Most Homes Forget

September 2026 · 6 min read · Home

Most of us know roughly when the milk goes off. Far fewer know when the smoke alarm in the hallway does. Yet the equipment that protects a home from fire and carbon monoxide wears out too, and unlike milk, it gives almost no sign that it has.

This guide covers the safety items that quietly carry a replace-by date, how long each typically lasts, and a simple way to make sure none of them lapses without you noticing.

Why safety equipment expires

Safety devices rely on parts that degrade with time. Smoke alarm sensors become less sensitive as dust and age take their toll. Carbon monoxide sensors are built to last a set number of years. Fire extinguishers can lose pressure. Sterile dressings stop being sterile, and medicines lose strength. None of this is visible from the outside, which is exactly why a date matters more than a glance.

The replace-by checklist

Typical lifespans vary by model and country, so always check the manufacturer's label or manual first. As a general guide:

Smoke alarms: test monthly, replace at ten years

Fire services in the UK and US give the same basic advice: test every smoke alarm once a month by pressing its test button, and replace the whole unit when it reaches the end of its life, usually after about 10 years. If your alarm uses replaceable batteries rather than a sealed long-life battery, change the battery at least once a year, or whenever it chirps.

If you move into a home and don't know how old the alarms are, look at the date on the back. If there isn't one, or it's older than 10 years, replacing them is cheap insurance.

Carbon monoxide alarms: the one people forget

Carbon monoxide has no colour, taste or smell, which is why an alarm is the only practical way to detect it at home. Fit one in any room with a fuel-burning appliance, such as a gas boiler, a wood burner or an open fire. In England, landlords have been required since October 2022 to fit carbon monoxide alarms in rooms with a fixed combustion appliance, other than gas cookers, and to repair or replace alarms when told they are faulty.

Carbon monoxide alarms usually last between 5 and 10 years. Many sound a distinctive end-of-life signal, but that's easy to confuse with a low battery. The safest approach is to note the replacement date the day you fit the alarm.

Fire extinguishers and fire blankets

Keep a fire extinguisher and a fire blanket near the kitchen, where most house fires start, and make sure everyone knows where they are. Once a month, check that the extinguisher's pressure gauge, if it has one, is in the green zone, that the safety pin and seal are intact, and that there are no dents or signs of damage. The label or manual will tell you whether it needs servicing and when to replace it.

If you ever use an extinguisher, even briefly, replace or recharge it. A partly used extinguisher may not work the next time you need it.

First-aid kits: check them twice a year

A first-aid kit is really a small collection of products, each with its own date. Plasters lose their stick, sterile dressings pass their sterility date, and painkillers and antihistamines expire. Twice a year, open the kit, replace anything past its date, and restock anything that was used.

Pay particular attention to emergency medicines. If someone in your home has a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector, an inhaler or other rescue medication, keep an eye on the expiry date and order a replacement in good time. Your pharmacist or doctor can advise on the right timing.

A simple routine for the whole house

  1. Walk the house once. Find every smoke alarm, carbon monoxide alarm, extinguisher, fire blanket and first-aid kit.
  2. Record each date. Write down the manufacture or replace-by date for each item, and where it is.
  3. Set reminders. Put the replacement dates somewhere that will remind you, not somewhere you'll have to remember to look.
  4. Test monthly. Press the test button on each alarm on the first day of the month. It takes two minutes.
  5. Review twice a year. Many people link it to the clocks changing: check the first-aid kit, the extinguisher gauge and any battery-powered alarms.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Removing the battery to stop a nuisance alarm. Move the alarm or fit a type less prone to cooking false alarms instead.
  • Assuming a working test means a working sensor. The test button checks the alarm sounds. An old sensor may still be less sensitive to real smoke.
  • Painting over or covering alarms. Paint and dust can block the sensor.
  • Forgetting the garage, loft conversion or holiday let. Every habitable area needs the same care.

The bottom line

Safety equipment doesn't announce when it has stopped doing its job, so the date is the only warning you'll get. An hour spent walking the house, recording dates and setting reminders is one of the most useful things you can do for your household's safety. Then test monthly and replace on time, and the equipment will be ready on the one day you need it.
