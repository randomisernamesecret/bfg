Best Handwriting Apps for Kids in 2026: iPad, iPhone and Apple Pencil

September 2026 · 9 min read · Education

Parents looking for a handwriting app face a crowded shelf. Most options look cheerful in screenshots, and most promise that your child will "learn to write" in a few taps. Very few explain what they actually check, how they charge, or what happens after the alphabet is done. This guide compares the handwriting apps parents, teachers and occupational therapists mention most often in 2026, with the strengths and trade-offs of each, so you can match the tool to your child rather than to the marketing.

What to Look For

Before the comparison, a short filter. A handwriting app worth your child's time should do most of these things well:

  • Real formation checking: does it notice where a stroke starts, which direction it travels and whether the letter is complete, or does any squiggle near the dotted line earn a star?
  • A sensible order: letters that share a movement (c, o, a, d, g) are easier to learn together than in strict A to Z order.
  • Feedback a child can act on: "start at the top" is useful; a generic cheer is not.
  • Works with a finger and a stylus: many families practise on a phone or a shared tablet without a stylus. A stylus should add something, not be required.
  • A path beyond single letters: words, sentences and, later, joined cursive are where handwriting becomes writing.
  • Child-appropriate privacy: no ads aimed at children, no unnecessary accounts, and purchases behind a grown-up check.
  • Clear pricing: you should know whether you are buying once, subscribing, or unlocking content piece by piece.
Key insight: An app that praises everything teaches nothing. Handwriting feedback only helps if the app can tell a well-formed letter from a mess, and is willing to say so kindly.

The Short List

Five apps cover most of what parents and teachers recommend for young children:

  • Writing Wizard
  • LetterSchool
  • iTrace
  • Wet-Dry-Try, from the Handwriting Without Tears curriculum by Learning Without Tears
  • Inkla

Each one has a clear audience and none is perfect for everyone. Prices and plans change often, so check the App Store listing before you buy; the notes below describe how each app charges rather than exact amounts.

Writing Wizard

Writing Wizard, from the French studio L'Escapadou, has been a default recommendation on "best kids handwriting app" lists for years. Children trace letters, numbers and words, and animated stickers and small games reward each finished letter. Parents can create custom word lists and choose between letter styles, and the same studio makes a separate Cursive Writing Wizard for joined handwriting.

Strengths: Friendly and motivating for young children, broad content (letters, numbers, words), custom word lists and a long track record.

Trade-offs: It is fundamentally a tracing app. It checks that the stroke followed the path, but it is forgiving, so it suits a four-year-old getting to know letter shapes better than a seven-year-old who needs to fix entrenched habits. On iOS, the full letter set is unlocked with a one-time purchase or a subscription.

LetterSchool

LetterSchool uses a three-stage method: tap the starting dots, trace the letter, then write it freehand. That progression mirrors how many paper programmes scaffold letter formation, and the animations are calm and polished.

Strengths: The staged approach is genuinely pedagogical, it covers print and cursive, and children enjoy it.

Trade-offs: Most content sits behind a paywall. LetterSchool offers a subscription as well as one-time unlocks for the full print or cursive alphabet, which is flexible but takes some reading to understand. Scoring is generous, and the app does not coach how hard a child presses.

iTrace

iTrace is popular with occupational therapists because of its depth. You can choose Zaner-Bloser, D'Nealian or Handwriting Without Tears letter styles, switch between left- and right-handed modes, add your own words, print worksheets and keep separate progress for several children.

Strengths: The most customisable app on this list, with print and cursive, English and Spanish alphabets, and multiple child profiles at no extra cost. It is a modest one-time purchase, with a free version to try first.

Trade-offs: The number of settings is a gift for therapists and a lot for a parent who simply wants to hand over the device. Feedback focuses on following the path rather than on pressure or rhythm.

Wet-Dry-Try (Handwriting Without Tears)

Handwriting Without Tears is a widely used paper curriculum, and Wet-Dry-Try is its official iPad app. It copies the curriculum's slate-chalkboard activity: the child watches a demonstration, then writes the letter three times, once with a digital sponge, once with a towel and once with chalk, while a coach called Marcy gives spoken feedback.

Strengths: The strongest link to a school curriculum on this list, with capitals, numbers and lowercase letters taught in the programme's own order.

Trade-offs: It is iPad-only and makes the most sense if your child's school already uses Handwriting Without Tears. Pricing is aimed at classrooms, with sample lessons to try for free.

Inkla

Inkla is our own app, so read this section with that in mind. It runs on iPhone and iPad, with a finger or an Apple Pencil. Children follow clear models for print and joined cursive, and every attempt is scored for shape, spacing, line control, pressure and rhythm. Common slips are named in plain words: if a child writes a mirrored b, the app says so and explains which side the stick belongs on.

What sets it apart in 2026 is language. The language a child practises can be different from the language the app speaks: English, French, German, Spanish, Romanian, Japanese hiragana, a starter set of 60 Simplified Chinese characters, or Hindi in Devanagari, each with its own guides and stroke order. A Word Explorer shows what every word and sentence means, with slow audio and pinyin, romaji or Hindi romanisation, and a ten-question Meaning Quiz brings words back on later days until they stick. Silly stories, postcards and comics give older children a reason to write whole sentences.

Strengths: Honest, specific feedback; print and cursive; eight writing languages with instructions in the child's own language; a parent dashboard with an optional PDF summary. It works offline, needs no account, keeps practice on the device and asks a grown-up before purchases or sharing. After a 7-day free trial it is a one-time purchase, with no subscription.

Trade-offs: On iPhone it runs in landscape only, because the writing area needs the width. Pressure coaching needs an Apple Pencil on iPad; with a finger, pressure is simply left out of the score. The Chinese set is a starter set rather than a full course, and the app is a practice tool, not a diagnosis. There is no Android version.

What Most Handwriting Apps Get Wrong

1. Rewarding scribbles

Many apps count any stroke near the letter as a success. Children learn by noticing and correcting mistakes; if the app never says "not quite, try starting at the top", there is nothing to correct.

2. Ignoring direction and start points

A letter can look right and still be formed wrongly. An "o" drawn clockwise or a "t" started at the bottom looks fine at age five and becomes a problem when writing needs to get faster. Good feedback watches the movement, not only the finished shape.

3. Stopping at the alphabet

Tracing 26 letters is the beginning. Children need words, sentences and eventually joined handwriting, plus reasons to write them. Apps that end at "z" leave families looking for the next tool.

4. Asking young children for accounts

A child practising letters does not need a cloud profile. Accounts add friction and privacy questions, and progress that stays on the device is usually all a parent needs.

Learning to Write Another Language

More families now want children to write a second language: the language grandparents speak, the one taught at weekend school, or simply one a child is curious about. Scripts such as Chinese, Japanese and Devanagari depend heavily on stroke order and direction, so tracing apps built only for the Latin alphabet cannot help. Look for an app that teaches the native stroke order but explains it in the language your child already reads. For teenagers and adults studying Chinese or Japanese seriously, a dedicated character trainer such as Skritter, a subscription service with far larger character sets, is the better long-term fit.

How to Pick

Under five and just meeting letters: Writing Wizard. It is friendly, broad and low-pressure.

Four to seven and you want a staged, playful path: LetterSchool.

Therapist, teacher or parent who needs fine control over letter style and settings: iTrace.

Your school uses Handwriting Without Tears: Wet-Dry-Try matches what your child sees in class.

You want honest feedback on iPhone or iPad, cursive as well as print, or practice in a second language: Inkla.

A Final Note on Paper

No app replaces a pencil and a ruled notebook. The real goal is transfer: writing clearly on paper, on demand, without guides. The approach that works for most families is a hybrid, a few focused minutes of app practice for feedback on formation, plus regular writing on paper for the feel of pencil on page. Be wary of any app that presents itself as a complete handwriting solution on its own.
