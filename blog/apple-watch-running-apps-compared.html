Best Apple Watch Running Apps in 2026, Compared

September 2026 · 8 min read · Fitness

The Apple Watch is now one of the most common running watches at any start line. Most people did not buy it for running. They bought it for notifications and health tracking, then found that it records a GPS run perfectly well. The real question is which app to run with. Apple's own Workout app has become far more capable over the last few years, and the third-party field has shifted too: Strava bought the coaching app Runna in 2025, and a handful of one-time-purchase apps now cover ground that used to need a subscription.

This guide compares six options on what matters during a run and after it: what the watch shows you, whether you can leave the phone at home, what is free, what costs money, and whether you can take your data with you.

What Matters on the Wrist

A running app lives or dies on a screen you glance at for half a second while breathing hard. Before looking at individual apps, it helps to agree on what good looks like:

  • Phone-free recording -- GPS, pace and heart rate from the watch alone, so you can run without carrying an iPhone
  • Readable metrics -- large numbers, sensible defaults, and ideally a choice over which fields you see
  • Useful feedback -- haptics and audio for splits, pace targets, intervals or warnings, so you rarely need to look at all
  • A sensible home for your data -- somewhere to review runs afterwards, plus export in standard formats such as GPX or FIT
  • Clear pricing -- knowing what is free, what is a one-off purchase and what is a recurring subscription

Apple's Workout App

The built-in Workout app is the baseline every other app has to beat, and it is a high one. It is free, it starts instantly, and it records your GPS route, pace, heart rate, cadence and elevation. Workout views are customisable: you choose which metrics appear and can add views for heart-rate zones, elevation, running power and, on recent models, running form metrics such as stride length, ground contact time and vertical oscillation. Custom workouts let you build warm-up, interval and recovery blocks, and the pacer and Race Route features help with target pace and routes you run often.

Recent versions of watchOS also added a Training Load view, which compares the effort of your last seven days with your longer-term baseline. Everything is stored in Apple Health, on your own devices, and in your iCloud account if you have Health sync turned on.

The gaps are in analysis and portability rather than recording. There is no social layer, route planning is limited, and Apple offers no direct GPX or FIT export, so moving runs elsewhere needs a third-party tool. For many runners, though, the Workout app is genuinely all they need.

Strava

Strava is the social network of running and cycling, and nothing else comes close to its community: the activity feed, kudos, clubs, segments and leaderboards. Its Apple Watch app records runs without the phone and shows the essentials -- pace, distance, time and heart rate. Most of what makes Strava distinctive happens once the activity reaches the phone or the web.

The free tier covers recording and the social feed. Segment leaderboards, deeper analysis and route tools need the subscription, which costs $11.99 a month or $79.99 a year in the US (prices vary by country). Beacon, Strava's live location sharing, is free from the phone app but remains a paid feature when you record from a watch. In April 2025 Strava acquired Runna, and a combined Strava + Runna plan now costs $149.99 a year in the US.

Choose Strava for the people rather than the watch screen. Plenty of runners record with a different app and let it upload to Strava, so they keep the social side without changing how they record.

Runna

Runna is a coaching app first. You give it a goal -- a first 5K, a marathon time, a comeback after a break -- and it builds a structured plan that adapts as you go, with sessions sent to the watch so intervals and target paces are guided on your wrist. It supports the Apple Watch alongside several other watch brands.

It is priced like personal coaching rather than a tracker: about $19.99 a month or $119.99 a year in the US. If a plan is what gets you out of the door, that can be good value. If you mainly want to record and review your runs, you would be paying for something you will not use.

Nike Run Club

Nike Run Club is still free, and its best feature has survived every redesign: Guided Runs. There are around 300 audio sessions, from easy recovery jogs to race-day simulations, voiced by coaches and athletes, and you can start them from the Apple Watch and listen through AirPods.

The watch display itself is simple and offers little customisation, and data export is limited, so moving years of history out later is awkward. For motivation, and for beginners in particular, it remains one of the best free options available.

WorkOutDoors

WorkOutDoors is the power user's choice and a long-standing favourite with trail runners and hikers. It is a one-time purchase of $8.99 and turns the watch into something close to a dedicated outdoor computer: more than 800 data fields, as many configurable screens as you want, vector and topographic maps on the wrist that can be stored for offline use, and navigation along planned routes.

The trade-off is complexity. Setting it up properly takes a while, and its strength is the watch itself rather than long-term training analysis on the phone. If you want maps and total control on your wrist, nothing else in this list comes close.

PaceGrid

PaceGrid is our own app, so read this section with that in mind. We have tried to be as plain about its limits as we have been about everyone else's.

PaceGrid records runs, rides, walks and hikes from the Apple Watch without the iPhone, and it can record pool swims on the watch too. The watch screen is deliberately simple: time, distance, pace or speed, and heart rate, in a fixed layout. You cannot customise it, there are no maps on the wrist, and there is no watch-face complication. Where it differs is Injury Guard, which taps your wrist during a workout when something looks off: heart rate above your ceiling, heart rate drifting upward, a pace that is fading, a session running past your duration limit, a hydration reminder and, if you switch them on, heat alerts.

After the run, the iPhone adds splits, personal records and matched routes, and the Training tab shows training load and recovery estimates, heart-rate zones and training plans. The iPhone app also handles saved routes, offline maps, Bluetooth heart-rate straps and compatible smart rings. You can export GPX and FIT files, import GPX, FIT and TCX files (including a zipped Strava export), and optionally upload finished workouts to Strava automatically or save them to Apple Health.

There is no PaceGrid account, and your workouts are stored on your device. Pricing is simple: everything is free for seven days, recording stays free after that, and one purchase with no subscription unlocks the full app, including your history, the training analytics and the Apple Watch app.

Training Load on the Wrist: Who Shows What?

Training load is a simple idea: compare what you have done recently with what your body is used to, and treat sudden jumps with respect. (Our guide to training load and injury risk covers the research and its limits.) Almost every serious app now calculates something like it. Far fewer put it where you can act on it.

  • Apple shows Training Load on the watch and iPhone, comparing your last seven days with your baseline. It is a review tool rather than a mid-run warning.
  • Strava keeps its deeper fitness and effort analysis on the phone and the web, largely behind the subscription.
  • Runna manages load through the plan itself, which is arguably the most practical approach if you follow a plan closely.
  • Nike Run Club and WorkOutDoors focus on the individual run rather than load across weeks.
  • PaceGrid splits the job. The training load and recovery analysis lives on the iPhone; on the watch you get live Injury Guard alerts while you run. These are estimates and prompts to ease off, not a diagnosis, and no app can promise you will stay injury-free.
Practical tip: Whatever app you use, a weekly look at how this week compares with your last month does more good than any single metric. If the jump is large, make the next few days easy.

Privacy: Who Holds Your Runs?

GPS tracks reveal where you live, where you work and when you are usually out. Before you choose, it is worth knowing where each app keeps them:

  • Apple Workout: stored in Apple Health on your devices, and in your iCloud account if Health sync is on.
  • Strava: uploaded to Strava's servers and shown according to your privacy settings. Check the defaults and set a privacy zone around your home.
  • Runna and Nike Run Club: linked to an account and stored with the company.
  • WorkOutDoors: records on the watch and saves workouts to Apple Health.
  • PaceGrid: no account, and workouts stay on your device. It goes online only to fetch map tiles, to build new routes, to upload to Strava if you connect it, and for heat alerts if you turn them on.

Which Should You Choose?

  • You want free and simple: Apple's Workout app. It is better than its reputation.
  • Your running friends are on Strava: Strava, possibly fed by a different recording app.
  • You want a plan to follow: Runna, or Nike Run Club's Guided Runs if you want coaching without a bill.
  • You want maps and total control on your wrist: WorkOutDoors.
  • You want live warnings and training-load awareness without a subscription, with your runs kept on your phone: PaceGrid.

Many runners combine two apps: one to record and analyse, and Strava's free tier for the social side. That setup costs little or nothing and keeps a copy of every run somewhere you control. If price is your main concern, our guide to running apps that don't charge monthly goes further.
