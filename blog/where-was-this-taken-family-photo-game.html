How to Play “Where Was This Taken?” With Your Own Family Photos

October 2026 · 7 min read · Parenting

Most family game nights run on the same three or four boxes in the cupboard. They are fine, but everyone already knows how they end. There is a better game hiding on your phone: show a photo from your own life, and let everyone guess where it was taken.

It needs no preparation, no questions to write and no box. It also produces something no bought game can: someone saying “wait, we went there?” and the next ten minutes of stories. This guide covers how to run it properly, especially when the players range from seven to seventy.

Why Your Own Photos Make a Better Quiz

A geography quiz is a test of what you studied. A game built on your own photos is a test of what you lived, and that levels the table in an interesting way. A ten-year-old can beat a parent because the parent was holding the camera, not looking at the street signs. A grandparent can beat everyone because they recognise the beach from forty years ago.

There is a second reason it works: the answer is never just a dot on a map. Every reveal comes with a story attached, and the story is the point. The score is only there to get everyone to lean in.

What You Need

  • One phone or tablet that holds a decent photo library. A shared family album works well if one exists.
  • A map that every player can use. A world map on the same device is ideal, because the guess and the answer then live in the same place.
  • Pen and paper if you are playing the low-tech version and keeping score by hand.
  • A bigger screen if you have one. Mirroring a phone to the television turns it into a room-wide event rather than a huddle over a small screen.

The Basic Rules

  1. Pick a photo no one has seen recently. Choose it at random, not because it is a favourite.
  2. Show it to everyone at once. No hints from whoever chose it.
  3. Everyone guesses in secret. Each player marks a spot on the map where they think the photo was taken, without the others seeing. Secrecy matters: without it, the last player simply copies the first.
  4. Reveal together. Show every guess on the map at the same moment, then the real location.
  5. Closest guess wins the round. Distance is the simplest fair measure.
  6. Talk before the next photo. This is the step people skip, and the one that matters most.

Five to fifteen rounds is a comfortable length. Fewer than five feels thin; more than fifteen and the youngest players drift off.

Setting It Up for Mixed Ages

The challenge with a family game is that you are never playing with equals. A few adjustments keep it fun for everyone.

Use a timer to keep it fair

A countdown stops one slow, careful player from holding up the table, and it stops the confident grown-up from thinking aloud and steering everyone else. Thirty seconds suits a quick game. Sixty is kinder for younger children and for anyone who needs to study the background of the picture. Ninety suits a relaxed evening with a lot of squinting at details.

Play in teams of two on one device

If your players range from five to adult, pair them up. A child and a grandparent, or a teenager and a younger sibling, share one pin and have to agree on it. The negotiation is half the fun, and nobody is left guessing alone at a map they cannot read yet.

Let children be the navigators

Children often know the map better than the adults do, because they have been looking at maps and globes at school more recently. Make their expertise official: they zoom, they place, and the adults explain the clues.

Handicap the strong players, gently

If one person always wins, try a rule such as “you only get twenty seconds” for the person who took most of the pictures. Nobody needs a trophy table of the same winner every week.

House Rules That Work

  • No googling, no scrolling back. Guessing from the picture alone is the game.
  • Clues are allowed after the guess. Once everyone has locked in, the photographer can tell the story. Before then, silence.
  • One “pass” per player. If a photo is awkward, such as one that shows someone who would rather not be remembered in that haircut, skip it without discussion.
  • Everyone gets a turn choosing the theme. “Only summers”, “only trips with grandma” or “only photos from before the little ones were born” all change the mood of the evening.
  • The winner of the round tells the story. It gives winning a small, friendly job.
A tip for sensitive photos: Skim the pool of pictures first, or pick specific years or albums to play from. A game night is not the moment to meet an old photo that nobody was ready to see.

Choosing the Right Photos

The best photos for this game are not the obvious landmarks. A picture of the Eiffel Tower is a gift to everyone. Better rounds come from:

  • A beach that could be one of fifty beaches.
  • A campsite, a cousin’s garden or a car park with a view in the background.
  • A hotel breakfast table. Somebody will recognise the curtains.
  • A day out that everyone remembers differently.

Because the photos come from your own life, a place that means nothing to a stranger can be a hard, funny and moving question for your family. Limiting the game to a single trip, a single summer or a single year gives it a shape and a story arc.

What If Most of Your Photos Are From One Town?

Many families photograph close to home. A game that only rewards continent-sized guesses would be pointless if your whole library is within thirty miles. The fix is to score by closeness relative to where your photos actually are, so choosing the right neighbourhood still counts. If you are keeping score by hand, agree up front on a few distance bands that fit your own area.

The Real Prize

You will not remember who won. You will remember that your daughter correctly placed a beach from when she was three, that your father thought a Lisbon street was Naples, and that someone said “I had completely forgotten that trip.” Keep the competition light and the conversation going, and the game does what it should: it gives your family a reason to look back together.
