The Best Stock Research Apps for DIY Investors in 2026

September 2026 · 9 min read · Finance

Buying a share has never been easier. Commission-free brokers, fractional shares and polished mobile apps have removed most of the friction from the trade itself. Understanding what you are buying is a different matter. Most apps will show you a price and a chart in seconds; far fewer tell you where their numbers come from, how old they are, or what exactly an "earnings per share" figure includes.

This guide compares six research apps a self-directed investor might reasonably use in 2026: Yahoo Finance, Seeking Alpha, Morningstar Investor, Koyfin, TradingView and our own app, Kanesh. It looks at what each is good at, where it falls short, what it costs and what it asks of you in return. Prices are US list prices checked in September 2026. They change often and vary by country, so confirm them before you subscribe.

What a Research App Should Do for You

Before comparing apps, it helps to separate research needs from features designed to keep you tapping. A DIY investor making considered decisions needs:

  • Fundamentals with their periods labelled. Revenue, margins, debt and cash flow over several years, with a clear note of whether a figure is annual, quarterly or trailing twelve months.
  • Sources and dates. Where a number came from and when it was last updated. A price-to-earnings ratio means little if you cannot tell whether it uses reported or adjusted earnings.
  • A route to the primary documents. Annual and quarterly reports, current reports and insider filings, ideally one tap away.
  • Macro context. Interest rates, inflation and employment affect whole markets, not just the company you are looking at.
  • A phone experience that respects your attention. Readable on a small screen, without a feed engineered to push you towards trading.

Yahoo Finance

Yahoo Finance is still the default starting point for many investors, and for good reason. The free tier covers more than 100 exchanges worldwide, with quotes, charts, key statistics, financial statements, news and portfolio tracking in one app. For a quick look at almost any listed company, it is hard to beat for breadth.

The trade-offs appear when you lean on it for decisions. Yahoo aggregates data from several providers, and it is not always obvious which one supplied a given figure or how it was calculated. The free experience carries advertising throughout. The paid tiers change the picture: Bronze ($7.95 a month, billed annually) removes ads and adds portfolio analysis and unlimited price alerts; Silver ($19.95) adds research reports and Morningstar ratings; Gold ($39.95) adds decades of downloadable history and advanced charting.

Best for: broad, free coverage and a fast first look at almost anything listed.

Seeking Alpha

Seeking Alpha's strength is its written analysis. Thousands of independent contributors publish detailed articles on individual companies, sectors and strategies, and some of them are genuinely excellent, written by people who know their industries well. Its quant ratings and factor grades offer a quick quantitative view, and earnings call transcripts are available for a large number of companies.

The limits are mostly about access and consistency. Much of the site sits behind Seeking Alpha Premium, which costs $299 a year with no monthly option; Alpha Picks, which adds specific stock recommendations, is a separate subscription. Article quality varies widely, and bullish and bearish pieces on the same stock can appear on the same day. You need an account for most features.

Best for: investors who want to read arguments for and against a stock, and are willing to judge the authors.

Morningstar Investor

Morningstar is best known for fund research, and Morningstar Investor brings its analysts' work to individual investors: fair value estimates, economic moat ratings, star ratings for stocks and funds, and a portfolio view that shows your real exposure across overlapping funds. The methodology is published, which makes the ratings easier to interpret than most black-box scores.

It costs $249 a year or $34.95 a month in the US. The emphasis is on long-term analysis rather than live market tools, and the depth of coverage is greatest for US-listed securities and widely held funds.

Best for: long-term investors, and anyone who holds several funds and wants to see what they add up to.

Koyfin

Koyfin started as a web-based alternative to professional terminals and has kept that feel: customisable dashboards, detailed charting, fundamentals, estimates and macro data, now with an iPhone app that syncs with the web version. For investors who like to build their own views of the market, it is one of the most capable tools available without an institutional budget.

The free plan is deliberately limited: two years of financials, two watchlists, two screens and two dashboards. Paid plans start at $39 a month (Plus) and $79 a month (Premium) on monthly billing, with discounts of up to 30% for paying annually. You need an account, and the app is at its best alongside the web version rather than on its own.

Best for: investors who want terminal-style depth and are happy to work mostly on a larger screen.

TradingView

TradingView is the leading charting platform for retail traders. Its charts are fast, flexible and consistent across web and mobile, with a huge library of indicators and a community that publishes trading ideas. It also includes screeners and a growing amount of fundamental data.

The free Basic plan shows ads and limits you to one chart per tab and two indicators per chart. Paid plans start at roughly $13 to $15 a month depending on billing. The community ideas are best treated as opinions: they are short, often technical, and rarely cite primary sources.

Best for: chart-led investors and traders.

Kanesh

Kanesh is our own app, so weigh this section accordingly. It is an information app for iPhone and iPad rather than a terminal, and its tagline is sourced alerts for your watchlist and the economy. It sends alerts for new SEC filings, insider purchases reported on SEC Form 4 and price crossings on your watchlist, and it shows Recession Watch (a model probability of a US recession within 12 months, from the yield-curve model published by the New York Fed, shown with how often it has been right) and the bond market. You can also ask a question in plain English and it finds the company, fetches the data and shows a card that links to its source, with the date of its data. Prices appear in your own currency (London pence, Tokyo yen and US dollars side by side), company financials and 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K filings come straight from SEC EDGAR, and you can keep a watchlist of stocks, ETFs, funds, cryptocurrencies or commodities (five tickers on the free app, with price alerts). All the AI runs on your device. There is no account, no tracking and no advertising.

It is clear about what it is not. Kanesh is not a brokerage, has no stock screener, no analyst ratings and no contributor community, and its prices come from Yahoo Finance, whose coverage and delays vary. Alerts are checked when iOS allows, so they are information and can arrive late. Treasury and Bureau of Labor Statistics data need no key; extra economic series need a free FRED key and earnings dates need a free Finnhub key, which you add yourself. New users get Kanesh Pro features for 7 days with no payment details; after that Pro is a one-time lifetime purchase or a monthly subscription, and adds an unlimited watchlist, alerts for new SEC filings, insider purchases, earnings dates and changes in recession indicators, recession history since 1960, an exposure map, comparisons, a daily brief, event prep, theses and a journal.

Best for: investors who follow a handful of companies on their phone and want the filings and sources close at hand.

Building a Research Stack

No single app does everything well, and most experienced DIY investors combine two or three. A sensible stack separates four jobs:

  • News: a reputable financial news source you actually read, rather than a feed that refreshes every few seconds.
  • Analysis: one tool for fundamentals and charts, chosen for how you invest: Morningstar for funds and long horizons, Koyfin or Yahoo Finance for broad data, TradingView for charts.
  • Primary sources: SEC EDGAR for filings, company investor relations pages for results and presentations, and FRED for economic data. Our guide to reading a 10-K without a finance degree is a good place to start.
  • Execution: your broker, kept separate from research. A broker earns more when you trade more; a research tool should help you decide when not to.

Red Flags in Any Finance App

  • Unlabelled earnings figures. If an app shows "EPS" without saying whether it is reported (GAAP) or adjusted, compare it with the company's own filing before you rely on it.
  • Delays that are not disclosed. Many free quotes are delayed. That rarely matters for long-term investors, but the app should say so.
  • Summaries without sources. Automated summaries are convenient, but a summary that does not point to the document it summarises cannot be checked.
  • Nudges to trade. Streaks, confetti and "top movers" alerts are designed to increase activity, not the quality of your decisions.
  • A shrinking free tier. Check exactly what the subscription adds before you pay for it, and whether a free primary source already gives you the same information.

The Bottom Line

The best research app depends on how you invest. Yahoo Finance remains the broadest free option if you can live with the ads. Seeking Alpha suits investors who want to weigh written arguments. Morningstar Investor is strongest for funds and long-term valuation work, Koyfin for terminal-style dashboards, and TradingView for charts. Kanesh is a lighter phone companion for investors who want alerts, the filings and the sources in view.

Whichever you choose, keep one habit: before you act on a number, find where it came from. If an app makes that hard, treat its numbers as a starting point, not an answer. Our guide to verifying financial data walks through a simple routine.
