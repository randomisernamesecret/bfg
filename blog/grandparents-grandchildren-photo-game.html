A Screen Activity Grandparents and Grandchildren Can Actually Share

October 2026 · 7 min read · Parenting

Screens tend to separate the generations. The children are on tablets in one corner, the grandparents are talking in another, and everyone calls it a nice visit. It is not that nobody wants to share time; it is that few activities suit a seven-year-old and a seventy-seven-year-old equally.

A photo guessing game is one of the exceptions. Each side has something the other lacks, and the screen is just a shared window onto the family’s own history.

Why It Works Across Generations

In a typical video game, children win by a distance. In a general-knowledge quiz, whoever read the most wins. A game built on your own photographs is different because the knowledge is split between generations.

  • Grandparents know the old places. They recognise the harbour, the seaside boarding house, the town before it was rebuilt, the car in the background.
  • Grandchildren know the map. They are fast at zooming, placing a pin and working out where a coastline probably is.

That balance makes a natural team. Neither can win alone, and each is quietly impressed by the other, which is a rare thing in a house with a screen in it.

How to Play Together

One device, passed around or shared

A single phone or tablet works well because it keeps people physically close. Put it on a table or mirror it to the television so that nobody has to squint at a small display. Large text and a big map matter more with older eyes than any rule.

Mix the teams

Pair each grandparent with a grandchild and have them agree on a single pin. The younger player handles the map, the older one supplies the clues. Teams of two on one device are the simplest way to let a six-year-old and an eighty-year-old compete on equal terms.

Use a timer kindly

A countdown helps the game move, but too short a timer shuts out the people who most need time to recognise something. Sixty or ninety seconds is gentler than thirty. If no one is in a hurry, play without one.

Keep the rounds short

Five rounds is a good opening game. Older players enjoy a shorter session and a second helping later; children enjoy leaving while they are still winning.

Conversation Prompts for Every Reveal

The best part of the game is what happens after the reveal. When the real location appears, ask something open. A few that tend to work:

  • “What was that day like?”
  • “Who else was there?”
  • “What were we doing before this was taken?”
  • “What did that place look like when you were my age?”
  • “Do you remember what we ate?”
  • “Whose idea was it to go there?”
  • “What was the funniest thing that happened on that trip?”

Do not force the stories. Often a single question is enough, and the grandparent will go on for five minutes. Let it. The game is the excuse; the stories are the point.

Tips for a Visit or a Holiday Gathering

  • Choose a quiet moment. The hour after a meal, when plates are cleared but nobody has left the table, is ideal.
  • Pick photos that include them. Trips the grandparents were on, or the grandchildren’s first beach, work better than pictures they have no connection to.
  • Choose one theme per visit. “Summer 2015” or “the year we went to Spain” keeps the game focused and makes it feel like a ritual.
  • Mirror it to the television if there are more than four of you. A shared big screen turns it into a family event rather than a huddle.
  • Include the in-between generation. Parents are often the ones who know the least about the old photos. They can learn from both sides.
  • Be ready to stop. If one photo opens something tender, leave the game and talk. Nobody needs to finish the round.

Playing With Older Albums

Some of the best pictures are not on a phone at all. They are in a shoebox, an album with sticky pages or a drawer in someone’s house. You can bring them into the game:

  1. Scan or photograph the prints. A phone camera in good, even light is enough for this. Take each one flat and straight on.
  2. Add them to your photo library. They will land as new pictures with today’s date.
  3. Set the location. Apple’s Photos app lets you adjust where a picture was taken, and you can choose the date as well. Ask the grandparents to tell you where, and write it in while they are sitting next to you.
  4. Put them in an album. A named album such as “Grandad’s photos” makes them easy to choose when you play.

This turns a quiet afternoon with a box of prints into a project the whole family can do together, and it protects those pictures from fading further. Because only photos with a location are used in the game, adding the place is what makes them playable.

A small gift: Ask older relatives to dictate a sentence or two about a photograph while you scan it, and keep the notes with the album. In twenty years, that will be worth more than the picture.

What You Are Really Doing

Grandparents do not usually want another gadget. They want time with their grandchildren, and a way to pass on what they remember. A shared game of “where was this?” gives both: the family looks at something together, and the stories come out without anyone having to ask for them. It is a screen activity that sends people toward each other instead of away.
