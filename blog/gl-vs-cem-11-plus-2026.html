GL vs CEM in 2026: Which 11+ Test Will Your Child Sit?

September 2026 · 7 min read · Education

For years, the first question parents asked about the 11+ was "GL or CEM?" The answer decided which books to buy, which question types to practise and how much to worry about time pressure. In 2026 the question still gets asked, but the answer has changed. CEM's paper 11+ has disappeared from grammar schools, GL Assessment now sets the test in most areas, and a few schools have moved to newer providers or write their own papers.

This guide explains what changed, what each current test looks like, and how to find out which one your child will actually sit.

What Happened to CEM?

CEM, the Centre for Evaluation and Monitoring, began at Durham University and has been part of Cambridge University Press & Assessment since 2019. For about a decade its 11+ was used by grammar schools in areas such as Birmingham, Buckinghamshire and Berkshire. It had a reputation for being harder to tutor for: mixed papers, a heavy vocabulary load and more questions than most children could finish.

Over 2022 and 2023, CEM stopped supplying a paper 11+ and moved to computer-based testing. Running an on-screen test for thousands of children at once was out of reach for most state grammar schools, so the areas that used CEM switched providers. The King Edward VI grammar schools in Birmingham moved to GL Assessment from the test sat in September 2022, and Slough followed in September 2023. Buckinghamshire's Secondary Transfer Test is now set by GL Assessment too.

CEM's assessment lives on as Cambridge Select Insight (previously CEM Select), a computer-based test used mainly by independent schools. So if you see "CEM-style" practice materials for sale, check whether your target school uses anything like it before you buy.

GL Assessment: Now the Default

GL Assessment sets the 11+ for most grammar school areas in England, including Kent, Buckinghamshire and Birmingham. It isn't one fixed paper. GL builds a test for each area or consortium, so the subjects, number of papers and timings vary from place to place.

What GL papers usually look like

  • Subjects: some mix of English, maths, verbal reasoning and non-verbal reasoning, depending on the area. Some tests include spatial reasoning or a short writing task.
  • Format: mostly multiple choice, often with a separate answer sheet, so children need to mark answers carefully in the right place.
  • Question types: well established and widely documented. Verbal reasoning, for example, draws on a familiar set of around 21 question types.
  • Timing: papers of roughly 45 to 60 minutes. The pace is brisk, but most prepared children can reach the end.
  • Practice material: familiarisation booklets are published for many areas, and most commercial 11+ books follow GL-style formats.

Because the question types are predictable, GL tests respond well to steady, systematic practice. Once a child recognises each format, most of the remaining work is accuracy and pace.

Most GL areas report age-standardised scores, which adjust for the fact that a child born in September has had almost a year longer to learn than one born in August. The qualifying score is set locally, and in some areas places then go by rank or distance among the children who qualify.

The Newer Options: FSCE and Consortium Papers

FSCE (Future Stories Community Enterprise) is a not-for-profit set up by Reading School, which writes an 11+ for grammar schools. It's used by Reading School, Chelmsford County High School for Girls, Colyton Grammar School and several schools in Yorkshire and Lancashire, and more have announced plans to adopt it for future entry years.

FSCE papers are based on the Key Stage 2 national curriculum, English and maths above all, up to the end of Year 5. Instead of stand-alone verbal and non-verbal reasoning sections, they tend to test reasoning through curriculum content, mixing multiple-choice questions with short written answers, and some schools add a creative writing task. Formats aren't published in the same detail as GL's, so the schools' own familiarisation material matters even more.

Consortium papers are another route. The Consortium of Selective Schools in Essex (CSSE), for example, writes its own English and maths papers for its member grammar schools, each lasting 60 minutes.

How to Find Out Which Test Your Child Will Sit

Don't rely on what a friend's child sat a few years ago, because providers have changed and may change again. Instead:

  • Read the admissions page of each school you're considering. Most name the test provider, the subjects and the test date.
  • Check your local authority or consortium, which often runs registration and publishes familiarisation material.
  • Read the registration documents carefully. They confirm the format for your child's year, and they're the source to trust if anything conflicts.
  • Use parent communities with care. The Eleven Plus Exams forum at elevenplusexams.co.uk is a useful place to compare notes, but always confirm details with the school.

While you're there, note the key dates. Registration for grammar school tests usually closes in the spring or summer of Year 5. The test itself is most often in September of Year 6, although some schools are moving theirs to the summer term of Year 5, and results normally arrive in October, before the national deadline for secondary school applications on 31 October. Missing registration is the one mistake no amount of preparation can fix.

How to Prepare for Each

For a GL test

Work through each question type systematically, starting untimed and building speed later. Use the area's familiarisation booklet early so the answer sheet and layout feel normal. From the spring of Year 5, add timed papers every week or two and review mistakes together. Verbal reasoning deserves steady attention because it's rarely taught in school, and a strong vocabulary underpins it, so keep your child reading widely.

Practising the GL-style subjects: Cognithix, which we make, covers English, verbal reasoning and non-verbal reasoning in short daily sessions on iPhone and iPad, with timed full papers, half papers and speed rounds. It doesn't cover maths, so pair it with maths practice if your child's test includes it.

For an FSCE or curriculum-based test

Make sure Year 5 English and maths are secure, especially arithmetic, fractions, grammar and punctuation. Practise explaining answers in writing, not just picking options, and read a wide range of fiction and non-fiction to build vocabulary and comprehension. Reasoning practice still helps with problem-solving, but it's less central than it is for a GL test.

For an independent school test

Check whether the school uses Cambridge Select Insight, the ISEB Common Pre-Tests or its own papers, and whether the test is on screen. Practice with typing, on-screen questions and timed sections helps children feel at ease on the day.

Common Myths, Updated for 2026

"Practise CEM-style papers just in case." For state grammar schools this no longer makes sense, because none of them use the old CEM paper. Unless your target independent school uses Cambridge Select Insight, time is better spent on the format your child will actually meet.

"GL is easy because it's predictable." Predictable isn't the same as easy. Popular grammar schools are heavily oversubscribed, so the scores needed for a place can be high, and small slips under time pressure add up. Accuracy and pace still matter.

"You can buy the real past papers." Test providers rarely release real past papers. What you'll find are familiarisation booklets from schools and consortia, plus publishers' practice papers written in the same style. Use the official familiarisation material first, because it shows the real layout and answer sheet.

"Reasoning practice only matters for GL." Even where a test has no separate reasoning paper, the habits that reasoning practice builds, such as spotting patterns, reading instructions carefully and checking an answer against the question, help across English and maths.

One Last Thing

It's easy to treat the choice of test as the thing that decides everything. In reality, the skills underneath are the same everywhere: a wide vocabulary, confident arithmetic, careful reading and the ability to think clearly under a bit of time pressure. Find out which test your child will sit, shape your practice around its format, and put the rest of your energy into keeping your child curious, rested and confident.
